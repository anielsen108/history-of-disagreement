Method & caveats

About this taxonomy

Two axes sort the field: what a method is for, and where a view locates the trouble

Methods are classified by telos, meaning what the exchange is for. Methods with the same moves can serve different ends. Vāda and jalpa share the five-membered syllogism and differ only in whether the debaters seek truth or victory. Prasaṅga and vitaṇḍā are formally the same act, refutation without counter-thesis, but one aims at liberation and the other at defeat. Classifying by telos instead of technique makes those kinships visible.

Views are classified by domain, meaning where the disagreement is said to live. Eight of the nine domains name a place where the trouble lies: belief, language, power, value, interest, the relationship, the psyche, and one’s relation to the absolute. The generative domain holds views for which strife is no trouble at all, because it drives thought and history forward. Teloi and domains are listed in the order that keeps threads in the diagram from crossing, with the generative domain held last. The order ranks nothing.

Methods are also grouped by family, the lineage or institutional milieu, for the lineage map. Family is a historian’s grouping and telos a philosopher’s. The two cut across each other on purpose.

Four conventions govern dates, lineage, presupposition and quotation

Dates. Each item carries a plotting year and an honest range. Ancient Indian and Chinese dates are contested by centuries. The Nyāya-sūtra is placed at c. 150 CE and the Aṭṭhakavagga at c. 400 BCE as conventional midpoints. The site makes no claim for either date.

Lineage. An edge means “descends from or defines itself against”. Hostile descent is still descent. The experimental matter of fact could not have existed without the disputation it was built to escape.

Presupposition. A method “presupposes” a view if its procedure only makes sense on that diagnosis. Diagrams and tables put the view first, on the left or down the side, and the methods built on it second. One view per method is marked primary. These assignments are this site’s arguments. The entries’ notes flag the most contestable ones.

Quotations come from the cited translation. Where an attribution is weak (for instance, “the disagreement of my community is a mercy”), the entry says so.

Flags and named readings mark how secure each claim is

Every entry carries two flags. The first says how secure its date is: firm, approximate, or contested. For methods, a second flag says how secure the pairing with its primary view is. “Stated by the tradition” means the practice itself names the diagnosis, as Fisher and Ury name interests. “This site’s reading” means the pairing is an interpretation. “Contested” means scholars disagree about it, and the diagram draws such threads dashed. The method sketches in the gap gallery are this site’s proposals, and no tradition practised them. The critiques on each method come from named critics. They are summarised unless quotation marks show otherwise.

About thirty of the most contested entries also carry a “Whose reading?” box. It lists the claims scholars dispute and gives each one a status. “Attested” means the primary source says it, with the passage cited. “Consensus” means specialists broadly agree, with one representative work named. “A named scholar’s reading” gives the scholar and the work, and names a dissenter where there is one. “This site’s reading” marks the site’s own judgment.

The style sheet applies to every page

Terms
A method is a way of conducting a disagreement. A view is an account of what a disagreement is about. A method’s diagnosis is the view it presupposes. In its own voice the site does not use “approach”, “practice” or “therapy” as synonyms for method. “Procedure” means a method’s steps.
Sentences
One claim per sentence. The agent comes first and the verb soon after. Active voice unless the agent is unknown. At most one hedge per claim, placed where the doubt lies. No rhetorical questions in the site’s own voice.
Words
Plain verbs (“show”, “use”), the thinker’s own term with a gloss at first use, and no evaluative adjectives that the evidence does not earn.
Tense
Present tense for what a text says (“Sextus argues”). Past tense for events (“Carneades argued for justice one day”).
Dates
BCE and CE for years before 1000; bare years after. “c.” marks an approximate date. Each entry gives a plotting year and the honest range.
Transliteration
IAST for Sanskrit and Pāli, pinyin for Chinese (tones on glossary headwords only), a simplified ALA-LC for Arabic, and common English forms for Hebrew terms (machloket, havruta).
Citations
Chicago author-date in the bibliography. Ancient texts by standard reference (Stephanus, Bekker, Diels–Kranz, Talmud folio, sutta number). Quotations come from the translation cited with them.
Paragraphs
No paragraph over about 120 words. Headings in running prose state a claim.
Checking
node scripts/lint-prose.mjs checks every page and data file for banned words, em dashes, rhetorical questions, stacked hedges, “not X but Y” constructions, long sentences and long paragraphs. It also stands in for a read-aloud test. It flags three sentences in a row that open on the same word, and four very short sentences in a row.

The data is free to reuse

The full dataset is downloadable as JSON and CSV from the data page, under CC BY 4.0. Corrections are welcome through the link at the foot of each entry, which opens a pre-filled GitHub issue.

Coverage after 1500 leans Anglophone, and several traditions are missing

Coverage after 1500 is weighted toward the Anglophone world. Practices not yet entered include Haudenosaunee condolence and council procedure, Ifá divination as a dispute-ending authority, Japanese nemawashi, Byzantine theological synods, Persian munāẓara poetry (the debate-poem genre), Hawaiian hoʻoponopono, and the Chinese examination essay. Each deserves an entry, and each would test the axes.

This taxonomy takes a side in what it classifies

A taxonomy of disagreement takes a position inside the disagreement it classifies. This one favours decomposition: find the locus, separate the strands, name the domain. That is the move shared by stasis theory, the distinguo, Chalmers’ elimination and the double crux. A Zhuangzian would decline the chart. A Mouffean would note that a chart which makes every conflict legible as a misplaced category has already sided with consensus. The map is offered as a tool. It does not settle what disagreement is.
