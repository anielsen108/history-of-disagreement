Taxonomy

This page draws the whole survey as a map. Use it when you want to see how the methods and the views connect.

How this bears on the thesis: every thread in the first diagram is one method’s assumption about what disagreement is. The thesis

the present

Methods are grouped by aim, which the traditions call telos: what the exchange is for. Views are grouped by where they say the dispute lives, which this site calls the domain. A square marks a method or an aim, and a circle marks a view or a domain. Each thread joins a method to a view it presupposes and takes the colour of the view’s domain.

The diagram opens with every group shut and with primary threads only, so the first screen shows which aims lean on which domains. A wider band carries more methods. Click a group to open it. Click a name to focus it, and click it again, or press “Read entry”, to open it. “Show all links” adds each method’s secondary views. A dashed thread marks a primary pairing that scholars contest. The slider hides methods and views dated after the chosen year, so pressing Play builds the field century by century.

One lane per family of methods. An edge means the later method descends from the earlier one or defines itself against it, as the elenchus against antilogic or the experimental matter of fact against the disputation. Each edge takes the colour of the later method’s aim. Hover a method to light its full ancestry and descent, and click it to open the entry. The filters above dim the methods they exclude. The axis gives half its width to the years since 1900, where half the methods fall. Scroll sideways on small screens.

Views run down the side. Each cell names the methods with that aim that presuppose the view. ★ marks a method’s primary view.

Where the dispute lives, by aim

Each bar is a life, coloured by the aim of the person’s main method, or by the domain of their main view if they gave no method. A thread runs from a thinker to each later thinker who read, studied with or wrote against them. Hover to light a person’s sources and heirs, and click to open their entry.

Lifetimes before about 500 CE are approximate and drawn paler. The time axis is compressed for antiquity and stretched for the modern period, as on the lineage map. Influence here is the documented kind: a named reading, a teacher, or a published reply. Diffuse cultural influence is left out. Scroll sideways on small screens.

By region

Each bar is one period; its segments give the share of that period’s methods whose diagnosis falls in each domain. Some periods hold only a few methods (one, for 1900–1950), so read their percentages as rough. Two things still show. Before 1800 no method locates disagreement in the psyche or in the relationship; epistemic, linguistic, power and contemplative diagnoses fill most of it. From 1950 to 1980 the psyche, interest and relational domains together hold 7 of 11 methods. After 1980 epistemic diagnoses hold 7 of 19 methods (37%), carried by the rationalist and deliberative methods, and relational diagnoses hold 5 of 19 (26%). That is the “therapeutic turn” discussed on the overview.

Chronology

Methods and views are interleaved here by date. The order shows when a diagnosis arrives before any method built on it, and when the reverse happens. Dates mark first articulation or attestation, and each entry gives its honest range.
