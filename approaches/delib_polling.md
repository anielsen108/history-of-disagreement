# Deliberative polling

- **When:** proposed 1988; first held 1994
- **Who:** James Fishkin
- **Telos:** Decision
- **Family:** Deliberative democracy & platforms
- **Presupposes:** [Burdens of judgment](../framings/burdens.md) (primary), [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md)
- **Descends from:** [Discourse ethics](discourse_ethics.md), [Parliamentary debate](parliament.md)
- **Gave rise to:** [Citizens’ assemblies](citizens.md), [Bridging systems](polis.md), [AI-mediated deliberation](ai_mediated.md)

Organisers poll a random sample of citizens, give them balanced briefing materials and competing experts, and have them deliberate in moderated small groups over a weekend. Then they poll the sample again. The output is a counterfactual: what the public would think if it had the chance to think. Fishkin treats some disagreement as a product of inattention and misinformation, which deliberation reduces. The rest is real, and the second poll reports it.

## Procedure
1. Random sample
2. Balanced briefing
3. Small-group deliberation; plenary with experts
4. Before/after measurement

## What it assumes
[Burdens of judgment](../framings/burdens.md): Reasonable people disagree without any of them being irrational or ill-willed.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Moderators and briefing writers.
- **Ends well when:** A measured, considered public opinion.
- **How it fails:** Deliberation favours the already-fluent.

**Strongest critique** (Lynn Sanders, ‘Against Deliberation’ (1997)): Deliberation’s norms of reasoned speech privilege those already advantaged. Testimony may be fairer than argument.
