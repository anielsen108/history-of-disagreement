# Syādvāda (sevenfold predication)

*anekāntavāda; saptabhaṅgī*

- **When:** anekānta early; saptabhaṅgī systematised c. 5th–8th c. CE
- **Who:** Jain logicians: Siddhasena, Samantabhadra, Akalaṅka
- **Telos:** Plurality
- **Family:** South Asian
- **Presupposes:** [Partial perspectives](../framings/perspectival.md) (primary)



Reality is many-sided (anekānta), so every assertion holds only ‘in some respect’ (syāt). The sevenfold scheme combines ‘is’, ‘is not’ and ‘is inexpressible’ under different standpoints (naya). Rival doctrines are recast as partial truths, each valid from its standpoint and false when absolutised. Jain authors used the parable of the blind men and the elephant to teach the point. The earliest surviving version is Buddhist (Udāna 6.4), where it mocks disputing sectarians.

## Procedure
1. Index each claim to a standpoint
2. Map rival claims onto the seven predications
3. Reject only the absolutising ‘only’

## What it assumes
[Partial perspectives](../framings/perspectival.md): Each party sees a real aspect of something larger.

## In practice
- **Third party:** None.
- **Ends well when:** Each claim is indexed to its standpoint; the ‘only’ is dropped.
- **How it fails:** Everything becomes partly true; no position can be wrong.

**Strongest critique** (Śaṅkara, Brahmasūtrabhāṣya 2.2.33): Contradictory attributes cannot belong to one thing at once. A doctrine of ‘maybe’ yields no knowledge.
