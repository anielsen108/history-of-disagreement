# Antilogic

*antilogikē; dissoi logoi*

- **When:** c. 450–400 BCE
- **Who:** Protagoras; the anonymous *Dissoi Logoi*
- **Telos:** Victory
- **Family:** Greek & Roman
- **Presupposes:** [Partial perspectives](../framings/perspectival.md) (primary), [Contest & honour](../framings/agon.md)

- **Gave rise to:** [Eristic](eristic.md), [Socratic elenchus](elenchus.md), [Deliberative rhetoric](rhetoric.md), [Pyrrhonian equipollence](pyrrhonian.md)

Protagoras was reported to be the first to say that on every matter there are two logoi opposed to each other. Antilogic is the drill that follows: argue the case, then argue its contrary with equal force. Its premise is the homo-mensura doctrine. If each person is the measure, disagreement is the normal condition of speech. The trained speaker learns to make the weaker logos the stronger.

## Procedure
1. Pair every speech with its counter-speech
2. Treat the audience as the measure of success
3. Exploit the reversibility of commonplaces (good/bad, just/unjust)

## What it assumes
[Partial perspectives](../framings/perspectival.md): Each party sees a real aspect of something larger.

## In practice
- **Third party:** The audience, who judge which logos prevails.
- **Ends well when:** The stronger speech wins the room.
- **How it fails:** Cleverness detached from any care for truth.

**Strongest critique** (Plato, Gorgias 462b–465d): Rhetoric of this kind is a knack of flattery. It is no art, and it produces conviction without knowledge.


## Whose reading?
- *attested*: Protagoras said that on every matter there are two logoi opposed to each other. Diogenes Laertius 9.51.
- *reading*: Antilogic follows from the homo-mensura doctrine. G. B. Kerferd, The Sophistic Movement (1981), ch. 6.


> One can read Plato’s project as a refusal of antilogic that keeps borrowing its tools.
