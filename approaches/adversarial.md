# Adversarial trial & cross-examination

*common-law adversary system*

- **When:** lawyerisation of English criminal trial from the 1730s
- **Who:** English criminal bar; Wigmore
- **Telos:** Decision
- **Family:** Parliament, court & debate hall
- **Presupposes:** [Collision with error](../framings/collision.md) (primary), [Contest & honour](../framings/agon.md)
- **Descends from:** [Stasis theory](stasis.md)


Truth is to emerge from a contest between partisan advocates before a passive judge and lay jury. Wigmore called cross-examination ‘beyond any doubt the greatest legal engine ever invented for the discovery of truth’. Langbein traces how defence counsel entered English felony trials only in the 1730s, and with them the modern theory that a contest between partisans finds the truth.

## Procedure
1. Each side presents its own case
2. Test the other’s witnesses by cross-examination
3. A neutral fact-finder decides

## What it assumes
[Collision with error](../framings/collision.md): Truth unchallenged becomes dead dogma.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Judge and jury.
- **Ends well when:** A verdict.
- **How it fails:** The better-funded advocate wins.

**Strongest critique** (Jerome Frank, Courts on Trial (1949)): The ‘fight theory’ of justice obstructs the ‘truth theory’. Partisan coaching distorts testimony.
