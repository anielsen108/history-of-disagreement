# Aristotelian dialectic

*dialektikē (Topics)*

- **When:** c. 350 BCE
- **Who:** Aristotle, *Topics* and *Sophistical Refutations*
- **Telos:** Formation
- **Family:** Greek & Roman
- **Presupposes:** [Disputed reputable opinion](../framings/endoxa.md) (primary), [Equivocation & verbal dispute](../framings/equivocation.md)
- **Descends from:** [Platonic dialectic](platonic_dialectic.md), [Eristic](eristic.md)
- **Gave rise to:** [Deliberative rhetoric](rhetoric.md), [Arguing both sides](utramque.md), [Jadal & munāẓara](munazara.md), [Scholastic disputation](disputatio.md), [Obligational disputation](obligationes.md), [Eristic dialectic](schopenhauer.md), [Toulmin model](toulmin.md), [Formal dialectic & dialogue types](formal_dialectic.md)

The answerer defends a thesis with yes-or-no replies, while the questioner tries to force a contradiction from endoxa, the opinions accepted by all, by most, or by the wise. The *Topics* catalogues the places (topoi) from which arguments may be drawn. Aristotle names three uses: intellectual exercise, encounters with ordinary people, and the philosophical sciences, where working through difficulties on both sides helps one see truth and falsity more easily.

## Procedure
1. Answerer defends a thesis by yes/no replies
2. Questioner seeks to extract the contradictory
3. Draw premises only from endoxa
4. Diagnose sophisms by type (language vs. outside language)

## What it assumes
[Disputed reputable opinion](../framings/endoxa.md): Some questions belong to things that ‘admit of being otherwise’, where only probable premises are available.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Sometimes an examiner or audience in the Academy’s exercises.
- **Ends well when:** The thesis survives examination, or the answerer is driven to contradiction.
- **How it fails:** Argument from opinion never touches the world.

**Strongest critique** (Francis Bacon, Novum Organum I.13–14 (1620)): The syllogism commands assent to propositions but not the thing itself. Discovery needs induction from experiment.



> The dialectical game survives into the medieval obligationes and modern dialogue logic.
