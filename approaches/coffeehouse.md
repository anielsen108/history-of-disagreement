# Coffeehouse & public sphere

*Öffentlichkeit*

- **When:** first London coffeehouse 1652; peak c. 1680–1730
- **Who:** London coffeehouses; Parisian salons; German Tischgesellschaften
- **Telos:** Understanding
- **Family:** Early-modern civic & scientific
- **Presupposes:** [Collision with error](../framings/collision.md) (primary), [Contested validity claims](../framings/validity.md)
- **Descends from:** [Free conversation](conference.md)
- **Gave rise to:** [Discourse ethics](discourse_ethics.md)

On Habermas’s account, private people came together as a public to argue about matters of common concern. Status was bracketed, at least in principle, and only the argument counted. The penny university and the periodical press formed one circuit. Critics note how much was excluded, but the ideal of rational-critical debate among equals sets the norm for later deliberative theory.

## Procedure
1. Bracket rank
2. Argue in public about common concerns
3. Circulate the argument in print

## What it assumes
[Collision with error](../framings/collision.md): Truth unchallenged becomes dead dogma.

## In practice
- **Third party:** None; the print public.
- **Ends well when:** Public opinion formed by argument.
- **How it fails:** Bracketing of status leaves status intact.

**Strongest critique** (Nancy Fraser, ‘Rethinking the Public Sphere’ (1990)): The bourgeois public sphere was built on exclusions of women, workers and others, who formed counter-publics of their own.


## Whose reading?
- *reading*: In the bourgeois public sphere, status was bracketed and only the argument counted. Jürgen Habermas, The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere (1962).
- *reading*: That sphere rested on the exclusion of women and workers, who formed counter-publics. Nancy Fraser, ‘Rethinking the Public Sphere’ (1990).
- *reading*: Actual coffeehouse talk was less open and less rational than Habermas’s ideal. Brian Cowan, The Social Life of Coffee (2005).
