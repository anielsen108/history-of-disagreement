# Truth commission

*truth and reconciliation commission*

- **When:** Argentina 1983–84; Chile 1990–91; South Africa 1995–98
- **Who:** CONADEP (Argentina); the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Desmond Tutu
- **Telos:** Understanding
- **Family:** Negotiation, mediation & peacemaking
- **Presupposes:** [Harm to relationship](../framings/harm.md) (primary), [The differend](../framings/differend.md)
- **Descends from:** [Restorative justice & circles](restorative.md)


After a period of state violence, a temporary official body takes statements from victims and perpetrators and publishes a record. It is not a court and passes no sentence. The South African commission could grant amnesty to perpetrators who disclosed their acts in full. Its report distinguishes four kinds of truth: forensic, personal or narrative, social, and healing or restorative. Victims testified in their own words and languages, in forms a court would not admit as evidence. Tutu described the work as restorative justice.

## Procedure
1. Take statements from victims and perpetrators
2. Hold public hearings where victims speak in their own terms
3. Offer conditional amnesty for full disclosure (South Africa)
4. Publish a report and recommend reparations

## What it assumes
[Harm to relationship](../framings/harm.md): Restorative justice sees conflict and crime as wounds in a web of relationships.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Commissioners, who hear testimony and write the record.
- **Ends well when:** A public record that victims recognise; amnesty or reparation.
- **How it fails:** Individual confessions stand in for a reckoning with the system.

**Strongest critique** (Mahmood Mamdani, ‘Amnesty or Impunity?’, Diacritics 32 (2002)): The commission defined victims and perpetrators as individuals who suffered or committed gross violations. The many who lost land and livelihood under apartheid law, and those who gained, fell outside its account.
