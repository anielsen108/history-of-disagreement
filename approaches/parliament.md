# Parliamentary debate

*debate under standing orders*

- **When:** Bill of Rights 1689; codified by Jefferson 1801, May 1844, Robert 1876
- **Who:** Westminster; US Congress; Henry M. Robert
- **Telos:** Decision
- **Family:** Parliament, court & debate hall
- **Presupposes:** [Faction & party](../framings/faction.md) (primary), [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md)
- **Descends from:** [Deliberative rhetoric](rhetoric.md)
- **Gave rise to:** [Competitive debate](debate.md), [Deliberative polling](delib_polling.md), [Agonistic pluralism](agonism.md)

A parliament debates under fixed rules and ends each debate with a division. Motions, amendments, speaking in turn and through the chair, and the ban on imputing motives to members all contain hostility without denying it. The ‘loyal opposition’ (Hobhouse, 1826) formalises disagreement as a permanent and legitimate office. Rules of order let a body decide while it remains divided.

## Procedure
1. Move; second; debate; amend; divide
2. Address the chair, not the opponent
3. Never impute dishonourable motive

## What it assumes
[Faction & party](../framings/faction.md): Hume sorts real factions into those from interest, those from principle (‘abstract speculative principle’, which he finds peculiar to modern times) and those from affection.

## In practice
- **Third party:** The Speaker or chair, who keeps order but does not decide.
- **Ends well when:** A division; the majority prevails.
- **How it fails:** Debate as theatre; decisions made elsewhere.

**Strongest critique** (Carl Schmitt, The Crisis of Parliamentary Democracy (1923)): Public discussion has become an empty formality. Parties and interests make the real decisions behind closed doors.
