# Eristic dialectic

*Eristische Dialektik*

- **When:** written c. 1830–31; published 1864
- **Who:** Arthur Schopenhauer
- **Telos:** Victory
- **Family:** Early-modern civic & scientific
- **Presupposes:** [Vanity & obstinacy](../framings/vanity.md) (primary), [Contest & honour](../framings/agon.md)
- **Descends from:** [Eristic](eristic.md), [Aristotelian dialectic](aristotelian_dialectic.md)


Schopenhauer gives thirty-eight stratagems ‘for being right’, with the opponent’s ruin as the explicit aim. The diagnosis is moral psychology. If people were honest, every debate would aim at truth. But innate vanity cannot bear to be wrong, so in most cases we argue for victory. The final stratagem, when all else fails, is to become personal.

## Procedure
1. Extend the opponent’s claim beyond its limits
2. Use homonymy
3. Provoke anger
4. Become personal (Stratagem 38)

## What it assumes
[Vanity & obstinacy](../framings/vanity.md): Most people cannot bear to be shown wrong.

## In practice
- **Third party:** None.
- **Ends well when:** Victory, by whatever stratagem works.
- **How it fails:** It works, which is the problem.

**Strongest critique** (Anatol Rapoport, Fights, Games, and Debates (1960)): Stratagems raise the opponent’s sense of threat and make them less able to hear. Victory in a debate persuades no one.



## Sources
- Schopenhauer, Arthur. *The Art of Controversy* (*Eristische Dialektik*, written c. 1830–31, publ. 1864). Translated by T. Bailey Saunders. London: Swan Sonnenschein, 1896.
