# Walking two roads

*兩行 liǎngxíng; 以明 yǐmíng*

- **When:** Zhuangzi, Inner Chapters, 4th c. BCE
- **Who:** Zhuangzi, ‘Qiwulun’
- **Telos:** Release
- **Family:** Chinese
- **Presupposes:** [‘This’ and ‘that’](../framings/shi_fei.md) (primary), [Attachment to views](../framings/attachment.md)
- **Descends from:** [Mohist disputation](bian.md)


Zhuangzi asks whether winning an argument makes the winner right, and answers that it does not. No third party can adjudicate, since any judge either agrees with one of us or with neither. The sage stops at the pivot of the Way (daoshu), lets ‘this’ and ‘that’ both proceed, and uses the light (yiming) of seeing that each ‘this’ is someone else’s ‘that’. The passage mocks Mohist disputation and also teaches a technique.

## Procedure
1. Notice each side’s ‘this’ (shi) is indexical
2. Stand at the hinge where both are allowed
3. Respond without fixing a position

## What it assumes
[‘This’ and ‘that’](../framings/shi_fei.md): Every ‘this is so’ (shi) is made from a standpoint, and from the other side it is ‘that’ (bi).

## In practice
- **Third party:** None; there is no neutral judge.
- **Ends well when:** Both sides allowed to proceed; the sage rests at the hinge.
- **How it fails:** Detachment that cannot act or decide.

**Strongest critique** (Xunzi, ch. 21 ‘Undoing Fixation’): Zhuangzi was fixated on Heaven and did not understand the human. Society needs names and decisions.


## Whose reading?
- *consensus*: Only the Inner Chapters (1–7) are usually ascribed to Zhuangzi himself. A. C. Graham, Chuang-tzŭ: The Inner Chapters (1981).
- *reading*: Zhuangzi is a perspectival relativist about shì and fēi. Chad Hansen, A Daoist Theory of Chinese Thought (1992); Graham (1981) stresses spontaneous response more than relativism.
