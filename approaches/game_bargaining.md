# Strategic bargaining

*game theory of conflict*

- **When:** Nash 1950; Schelling 1960; Raiffa 1982
- **Who:** John Nash; Thomas Schelling; Howard Raiffa
- **Telos:** Settlement
- **Family:** Negotiation, mediation & peacemaking
- **Presupposes:** [Mixed-motive game](../framings/strategic.md) (primary), [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md)

- **Gave rise to:** [Interactive problem-solving workshop](problem_solving.md), [Principled negotiation](principled.md), [Rapoport’s rules & region of validity](rapoport.md)

Game theory treats conflict as a mixed-motive game. The parties share an interest in agreement and differ on its terms. Nash derives a unique bargaining solution from axioms. Schelling shows that commitment, focal points and threats shape outcomes more than arguments do. Raiffa turns this into practical analysis of BATNAs and zones of possible agreement. The model treats disagreement as a structure of payoffs, and beliefs play little part.

## Procedure
1. Map payoffs and alternatives
2. Find focal points
3. Manage commitments and credible threats

## What it assumes
[Mixed-motive game](../framings/strategic.md): Schelling holds that few conflicts are pure.

## In practice
- **Third party:** None, or an arbitrator in some models.
- **Ends well when:** An equilibrium agreement inside the zone of possible agreement.
- **How it fails:** Values and identities do not show up as payoffs.

**Strongest critique** (Anatol Rapoport, Fights, Games, and Debates (1960)): Game theory models only one kind of conflict. Debates, where the aim is to change the other’s mind, fall outside it.
