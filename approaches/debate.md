# Competitive debate

*debating society; Oxford/Cambridge Union*

- **When:** London debating societies 1770s; Cambridge Union 1815; Oxford Union 1823; Lincoln–Douglas 1858
- **Who:** Debating societies; school and university leagues
- **Telos:** Formation
- **Family:** Parliament, court & debate hall
- **Presupposes:** [Contest & honour](../framings/agon.md) (primary)
- **Descends from:** [Parliamentary debate](parliament.md), [Deliberative rhetoric](rhetoric.md)


Sides are assigned, often by lot, and the speaker argues whatever they are given. Ticketed London debating societies of the 1770s–90s took up questions of politics, religion and the sexes. The Unions trained a governing class. The modern tournament formats (British Parliamentary, policy, Lincoln–Douglas) descend from in utramque partem. They prize advocacy over belief. Deborah Tannen counts this among the vices of an ‘argument culture’.

## Procedure
1. Sides assigned
2. Timed constructive and rebuttal speeches
3. Adjudicated by judges or audience vote

## What it assumes
[Contest & honour](../framings/agon.md): Argument is a form of combat whose stake is standing.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Judges or audience vote.
- **Ends well when:** A winner on points.
- **How it fails:** Training in advocacy without conviction.

**Strongest critique** (Deborah Tannen, The Argument Culture (1998)): Treating every issue as a two-sided fight polarises and hides the many-sidedness of real questions.
