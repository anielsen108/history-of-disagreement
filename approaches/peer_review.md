# Peer review

*refereeing*

- **When:** Royal Society Committee of Papers 1752; written referee reports from the 1830s; routine after 1945
- **Who:** The Royal Society; journal editors and referees
- **Telos:** Truth
- **Family:** Early-modern civic & scientific
- **Presupposes:** [Error & ignorance](../framings/error.md) (primary), [Unfinished inquiry](../framings/inquiry.md)
- **Descends from:** [Experimental matter of fact](experimental.md)


An editor sends a manuscript to a few specialists, who report on its faults in writing and usually without giving their names. The author must answer the reports before the journal prints the paper. The Royal Society asked for written reports on papers from the 1830s. Csiszar and Baldwin show that journals adopted the practice slowly, and that the phrase ‘peer review’ and its standing as a mark of scientific legitimacy date from the 1970s. The method assumes that authors cannot see their own errors and that qualified rivals can.

## Procedure
1. Editor chooses referees with competence in the field
2. Referees report faults in writing, usually unnamed
3. Author revises or rebuts each point
4. Editor decides

## What it assumes
[Error & ignorance](../framings/error.md): Two contradictory claims cannot both be true, so at least one party is wrong.

## In practice
- **Third party:** The editor, who chooses the referees and decides.
- **Ends well when:** The editor accepts the paper once the author has answered the reports.
- **How it fails:** Referees miss errors and favour known names and familiar results.

**Strongest critique** (Douglas Peters and Stephen Ceci, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 5 (1982)): Twelve published articles went back to the journals that had printed them, under invented names and institutions. Three were recognised, and eight of the other nine were rejected.
