# Integration

*constructive conflict*

- **When:** Mary Parker Follett, 1925
- **Who:** Mary Parker Follett
- **Telos:** Settlement
- **Family:** Negotiation, mediation & peacemaking
- **Presupposes:** [Constructive difference](../framings/constructive.md) (primary), [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md)
- **Descends from:** [Speculative dialectic](hegel.md)
- **Gave rise to:** [Mediation](mediation.md), [Principled negotiation](principled.md)

Follett defines conflict as ‘the appearance of difference, difference of opinions, of interests’, and she says we should use it. There are three ways to deal with it. Domination gives one side its way. Compromise makes each side give up something. Integration finds a solution in which ‘both desires have found a place’. In her library example, one reader wanted the window open and another wanted it shut, and opening a window in the next room satisfied both. Getting to Yes is largely Follett with case studies.

## Procedure
1. Bring the difference into the open
2. Break each demand into its constituent desires
3. Invent an option that satisfies both

## What it assumes
[Constructive difference](../framings/constructive.md): Follett asks us to think of conflict as friction in an engine.

## In practice
- **Third party:** Optional; usually the parties themselves.
- **Ends well when:** An integration in which both desires find a place.
- **How it fails:** Not every conflict can be integrated.

**Strongest critique** (Chantal Mouffe, On the Political (2005), applied here to Follett): Some conflicts are antagonistic. Treating them as integrable differences makes the stronger party’s frame the default.


## Sources
