# Court policy debate

*鹽鐵論 Yántiě lùn*

- **When:** 81 BCE
- **Who:** Han court officials vs. Confucian ‘worthies and literati’
- **Telos:** Decision
- **Family:** Chinese
- **Presupposes:** [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md) (primary), [Faction & party](../framings/faction.md), [What cannot be measured](../framings/unmeasurable.md)
- **Descends from:** [Rectification of names](zhengming.md)


In 81 BCE the Han court summoned officials and Confucian literati to debate the state monopolies of salt and iron, and Huan Kuan later wrote the debate up as a set-piece dialogue. On one side stood fiscal statecraft and frontier defence. On the other stood agrarian virtue and the Confucian distrust of profit. It is one of the earliest records of a structured policy debate between institutional factions before a sovereign arbiter.

## Procedure
1. Each faction speaks in turn before the throne
2. Arguments from precedent, classic and consequence

## What it assumes
[Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md): What people say they want, their positions, is a strategy for what they need, their interests.

## In practice
- **Third party:** The throne, which hears and decides.
- **Ends well when:** Policy decided by the sovereign.
- **How it fails:** The record is a partisan reworking.

**Strongest critique** (Michael Loewe, Crisis and Conflict in Han China (1974)): Huan Kuan wrote decades later as a sympathiser of the literati. The ‘debate’ is a literary construction.


## Whose reading?
- *reading*: Huan Kuan compiled the record decades after the debate of 81 BCE and favoured the literati. Michael Loewe, Crisis and Conflict in Han China (1974), ch. 3.


## Sources
- Huan Kuan. *Discourses on Salt and Iron*. Translated by Esson M. Gale. Leiden: Brill, 1931.
