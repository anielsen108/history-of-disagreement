# Mediation

*facilitative, transformative & narrative mediation*

- **When:** Pound Conference 1976; Bush & Folger 1994; Winslade & Monk 2000
- **Who:** Frank Sander; Robert Baruch Bush & Joseph Folger; John Winslade & Gerald Monk
- **Telos:** Settlement
- **Family:** Negotiation, mediation & peacemaking
- **Presupposes:** [Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md) (primary), [Relationship-level message](../framings/relationship_level.md), [Harm to relationship](../framings/harm.md)
- **Descends from:** [Integration](follett.md)


Sander’s 1976 address launched modern alternative dispute resolution. Its idea, soon named the ‘multi-door courthouse’, was to send each dispute to the process that fits it. Facilitative mediation seeks interest-based settlement. Transformative mediation drops settlement as the goal and aims at empowerment and recognition, so that each party regains clarity and sees the other. Narrative mediation, drawing on Foucault, helps parties deconstruct ‘conflict-saturated stories’ and co-author alternatives.

## Procedure
1. Neutral third party controls process, not outcome
2. Separate positions from interests (facilitative)
3. Follow the parties’ shifts in empowerment and recognition (transformative)
4. Externalise the conflict story (narrative)

## What it assumes
[Interests beneath positions](../framings/interests.md): What people say they want, their positions, is a strategy for what they need, their interests.

## In practice
- **Third party:** A neutral mediator who controls process, not outcome.
- **Ends well when:** A voluntary agreement; or, in transformative mode, empowerment and recognition.
- **How it fails:** Private settlement under power imbalance.

**Strongest critique** (Owen Fiss, ‘Against Settlement’ (1984)): Settlement trades justice for peace. Power imbalances go unremedied and public norms go unmade.
