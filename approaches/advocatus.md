# Devil’s advocate & red team

*advocatus diaboli; promotor fidei*

- **When:** office of Promotor Fidei 1587–1983; Janis 1972; military and security red teams
- **Who:** The Roman Congregation of Rites; Irving Janis
- **Telos:** Decision
- **Family:** Parliament, court & debate hall
- **Presupposes:** [Collision with error](../framings/collision.md) (primary), [Argumentative reasoning](../framings/argumentative.md)
- **Descends from:** [Scholastic disputation](disputatio.md)


Sixtus V gave the Roman Congregation of Rites an officer, the Promoter of the Faith, whose duty was to argue against every candidate for sainthood. Popular speech called him the devil’s advocate. A cause went forward only after its promoters had answered his objections. Mill cites the office in *On Liberty*: even the most intolerant of churches listens to a devil’s advocate before it canonises. Janis prescribed the same role as a remedy for groupthink, and armies and security teams keep red teams to attack their own plans.

## Procedure
1. Appoint someone whose duty is to argue against the proposal
2. Require the proposers to answer each objection
3. Decide only after the objections have been heard

## What it assumes
[Collision with error](../framings/collision.md): Truth unchallenged becomes dead dogma.

## In practice
- **Third party:** The tribunal or the commander, who hears both sides.
- **Ends well when:** The proposal survives its objections, or is dropped.
- **How it fails:** Everyone knows the objector is playing a part.

**Strongest critique** (Charlan Nemeth, Keith Brown and John Rogers, ‘Devil’s Advocate versus Authentic Dissent’ (2001)): Assigned dissent prompts less rethinking than dissent the speaker means. A group discounts an opponent who is known to be playing a role.


> John Paul II’s reform of 1983 ended the adversarial form of the office.
